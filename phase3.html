1.1 Timestamp granularity: the finding that shapes everything downstream¶

Date looks like a timestamp (2023-12-16 22:00:00 UTC, length 23 on every row) but 357,452 of 358,154 rows (99.8%) have minutes and seconds at 00:00, and 357,192 (99.7%) sit at hour 00. It is a date-only field in timestamp clothing.

Consequence: we cannot establish that a headline preceded an intraday move, so the alignment rule in section 4 has to be conservative.

2.3 Quality problems¶

Missingness. Publisher is empty on 225,187 of 358,154 raw rows (62.9%), which rules out publisher-based filtering.

Duplication and cross-attribution. The raw subset has 265,585 distinct headlines across 358,154 rows. Splitting that: 32,159 rows (8.98%) are true duplicates (same headline and ticker), while 60,410 (16.87%) come from one headline being filed under several tickers, one headline appears under 51 different symbols.

Mis-attribution. Of the 49,306 raw headlines containing a parenthesized ticker, 24,769 (50.2%) do not contain the filed Stock_symbol anywhere. Example: "NVIDIA (NVDA) Up 243% YTD: Will It Carry Momentum in 2024?" filed under AAPL.

Caveat: ticker-string matching undercounts relevance badly, since most headlines name companies in words ("Apple Unveils New iPhone"). The defensible claim is the 50.2% figure restricted to headlines that use ticker symbols. Company-name matching is Phase 4.

3. Preprocessing plan¶

Each step below exists because of a specific finding above, not as generic hygiene.

Step Driven by
drop Unnamed: 0 leftover pandas index column
drop 3 roundup patterns (5,116 rows, 1.43%) ticker-list headlines with dates off by 1-3 days
SELECT DISTINCT on (date, ticker, headline) 8.98% true duplicates
cast Date → DATE, discard the time 99.7% of timestamps are 00:00
carry symbol_in_title, do not filter on it 50.2% paren mismatch, but ticker matching undercounts relevance
restrict to top 25 tickers, 2019-2023 coverage density; 2021 dip documented
tokenize at max_length=64 p95 headline = 96 characters
score distinct headlines only avoids 29,605 redundant forward passes
minimum-article threshold per ticker-day (proposed) 33.7% single-article days

Deferred to Phase 4: company-name matching as a relevance filter.

4. Alignment rule and leakage control¶

Each article's publication date D maps to T0, the most recent trading day at or before D (DuckDB ASOF JOIN). The label is the return from close(T0) to the next trading day's close.

  • D is a trading day → we claim only close(D) → close(D+1). An article published mid-session cannot be credited with that session's move.
  • D is a weekend or holiday → T0 is the prior Friday, so the label is Friday close → Monday close, capturing the weekend-news effect with no leakage.

The trailing-volatility control in section 5.3 uses a 20-day rolling standard deviation computed through T0 only.

Join integrity: 113,075 rows in, 113,075 out. 10,574 articles (9.4%) published on non-trading days, mean lag 0.153 days.

5.3 Robustness: does it survive a volatility control?¶

The obvious objection is that volatility clusters, so news volume might just proxy for "this stock has been jumpy lately." We regress next-day absolute return on log1p(articles) and trailing 20-day realised volatility.

Trailing volatility is reconstructed from the committed returns table: the realised return on day t equals fwd_ret_1d at t−1, so a 20-day rolling standard deviation through T0 uses only information available at T0.

5.4 Horizon robustness: does a longer window rescue direction?¶

A reasonable objection to the null in 5.1 is that one day is too short, and sentiment may need several days to show up in price. We test 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 and 20 trading-day forward returns, compounded per ticker.

Direction stays null at every horizon. Raw agreement does rise with the window, but the always-up baseline rises faster because stocks drift upward, so the gap against baseline widens from -1.0 points at one day to -4.7 at twenty. Reporting accuracy without a baseline would have made this look like improvement.

The magnitude result holds at every horizon, which matters more than the original finding: a relationship that survives six window choices is not an artifact of one.

6. Model and evaluation plan¶

Baseline. TF-IDF over headlines + logistic regression. Cheap, interpretable, and the standard comparison point in the financial-NLP literature.

Deep model. FinBERT fine-tuned on the Financial PhraseBank sentiment corpus, then applied to FNSPID headlines. FNSPID carries no sentiment labels, so it cannot be used as fine-tuning data directly, a point worth stating plainly, since the Phase 2 draft implied otherwise.

Targets.

  1. Primary: next-day absolute return (magnitude). Section 5.2 shows a real, significant, volatility-controlled relationship here.
  2. Secondary: next-day direction. Section 5.1 shows this is near-chance; we report it as a measured null rather than quietly dropping it.

Metrics. Macro-F1 (not accuracy, 62.6% of headlines are neutral), precision and recall per class, Pearson and Spearman for continuous targets, plus per-ticker breakdowns because BIIB dominates the tails.

Validation. Time-ordered split, never random: train 2019-2021, validate 2022, test 2023. A random split would leak future information through overlapping news cycles and through the cross-filed headlines documented in section 2.3.

8. Summary¶

The pipeline runs end to end and the data is well enough understood to commit to a model plan. The central finding of Phase 3 is that our original Phase 2 hypothesis is not supported, daily sentiment does not predict next-day direction, and the sentiment sign performs slightly worse than always guessing up, while an adjacent and more tractable target is strongly supported: news volume predicts the magnitude of the next day's move, monotonically across four buckets, and the effect survives controlling for trailing volatility at t = 12.

Phase 4 therefore targets magnitude as the primary task, reports direction as a measured null, and adds company-name relevance filtering to address the label noise quantified in section 2.3.
